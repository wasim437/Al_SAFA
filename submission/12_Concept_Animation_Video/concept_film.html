On the shade figure

An earlier draft claimed 99.2% annual shade on the spine. It does not survive a geometric check: a flat 9 m canopy at 5.5 m stops shading a 9 m walkway once the sun is low and to the south, because the shadow slides off the path — which you can watch happen in scene three. Modelled across all 4,402 annual daylight hours, the canopy alone gives 60.2%, and the canopy with its flanking tree avenue gives 69.6%.

The honest number is lower, and it is the better argument: it is what proves the tree avenue is structural to the concept rather than decoration.

Verification of the film's solar model

The film computes sun angles in the browser; the project's figures come from pvlib running the NREL algorithm in Python. The two were checked against each other:

64.75°
Equinox noon altitude — Python gives 64.8°
0.19 m
6 m tree shadow at 88.2° — identical to Python
16.48 m
Same tree at 20° — identical to Python
4,364
Annual daylight hours — Python gives 4,402

The 0.9% gap in daylight hours is expected and explainable: pvlib reports apparent altitude, which includes atmospheric refraction and so keeps the sun visible a few minutes longer at each end of the day. The film uses true geometric altitude. Neither is wrong; they answer slightly different questions, and the difference never exceeds one degree.

Why the canopy alone is not enough

The same model, evaluated on the 9 m walkway under its 5.5 m canopy:

98%
21 June, noon — walkway covered
0%
21 December, 09:00 — shadow offset 9.06 m, entirely off the path

Watch it happen in scene three. This is why the tree avenue is structural, and why the honest annual figure is 69.6% rather than 99.2%.
